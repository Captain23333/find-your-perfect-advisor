# Evidence-grounded outreach guide

Use this guide to choose among competing outreach advice. Official program and
advisor instructions are requirements; university guidance is stronger than
platform anecdotes; LinkedIn and Reddit reports are useful for failure modes
and recipient preferences but do not prove a universal response-rate formula.

## High-confidence rules

1. **Check whether contact is useful or required.** Direct-supervisor systems,
   committee admissions, and rotations behave differently. Never infer the
   model from country alone.
2. **Make the target unmistakable.** Use the correct title, name, program,
   degree, and intake. Follow any lab-specific subject line or document list.
3. **Be concise and specific.** Put the purpose early and make the message easy
   to answer without scrolling through a personal statement.
4. **Demonstrate a connection.** Tie an inspected advisor paper/project to a
   real applicant question, method, dataset, or experience. Naming a paper is
   not evidence of understanding it.
5. **Use a low-friction request.** Availability or route confirmation is often
   easier to answer than a demanded meeting. A short conversation can be left
   open as an option.
6. **Keep the human owner.** AI may structure and edit, but the applicant must
   recognize every sentence as true and be able to discuss every claimed
   connection in an interview.

## Conditional choices

| Choice | Use when | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|
| Ask for a meeting | Direct-supervisor culture or advisor invites it | Committee/rotation program discourages pre-application meetings |
| Attach an RP | Official guidance requests it, proposal-led application, or advisor asks | First factual inquiry where only a CV is requested |
| Discuss funding | The advert/contact guide asks, or funding route is the decision | It would replace the actual research-fit purpose |
| Mention one paper | You inspected enough to state the connection accurately | Only title/metadata was seen |
| Include links | A relevant portfolio, Scholar, GitHub, or project is easy to inspect | A long list makes the message look like marketing |

## Quality checklist

- [ ] The subject states degree/intake or position and research area.
- [ ] The greeting uses the verified title and spelling.
- [ ] The first two sentences reveal purpose and target.
- [ ] The advisor-specific sentence could not be sent unchanged to another PI.
- [ ] The applicant evidence exists in the CV or user-provided facts.
- [ ] The connection explains how/why, not only that two topics are related.
- [ ] The email has one primary question or request.
- [ ] Attachments follow official instructions and have clear filenames.
- [ ] No result, publication status, award, skill, funding fact, or recruiting
      signal is exaggerated.
- [ ] No generic admiration, desperation, entitlement, or mass-mail phrasing.
- [ ] The applicant can explain every technical sentence without the draft.
- [ ] The email does not promise a reply or imply that silence is rejection.

## Common bad transformations

- `I read your paper` + copied abstract → no demonstrated engagement.
- Shared keyword → claimed research fit.
- Advisor uses method X + applicant wants to learn X → claimed prior expertise.
- Program page lacks funding details → "no funding available."
- No reply → advisor is uninterested.
- One platform user's response rate → universal sending schedule or formula.
- Polished generic prose → authenticity.

## Research basis

- Oxford advises a polite, concise, tailored email; it asks applicants to
  connect specific publications/facilities to their own experience and attach
  a CV and draft proposal when available rather than putting all detail in the
  body: https://www.ox.ac.uk/admissions/graduate/application-guide/starting-your-application/research-project-and-supervisor
- UC Davis faculty guidance recommends a brief message that introduces the
  applicant, asks about intake, shows why that faculty member, and attaches a
  CV; it also warns that no reply has many possible causes:
  https://lucklab.ucdavis.edu/blog/2018/9/17/emailing-faculty
- A current LinkedIn faculty discussion repeatedly rejects long, vague, or
  obviously AI-generated messages and asks for a clear purpose plus a real
  connection. Treat its numerical inbox and timing claims as anecdotal:
  https://www.linkedin.com/posts/dr-geoff-currie-28839329_good-advice-but-my-filter-is-even-simpler-activity-7473806270496460800-rltQ
- A Reddit faculty discussion shows the meeting request is not universal: some
  faculty will not meet before applications are reviewed, even when they reply:
  https://www.reddit.com/r/gradadmissions/comments/1mvy7j1/cold_emailing_professors_for_phd_what_do_you/
- A Reddit professor thread identifies the central AI failure as claiming a
  connection instead of demonstrating one:
  https://www.reddit.com/r/Professors/comments/1gvq108/how_im_handling_prospective_phd_student_emails/
- Two login-read Xiaohongshu examples independently favor specific research
  evidence and low-pressure intake questions; one also states that whether an
  RP belongs in the first email depends on the program's process. Treat their
  interview outcome and inbox-volume claims as individual experience:
  https://www.xiaohongshu.com/explore/6a09adce0000000035030f2a and
  https://www.xiaohongshu.com/explore/6a21ab32000000000702fe52
